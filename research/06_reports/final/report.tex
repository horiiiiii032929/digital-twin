\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Large language models can generate explanations, examples, and questions on
demand, but a course tutor has obligations that a general-purpose assistant
does not. It must answer from the correct course and source version, respect an
instructor's source permissions and teaching policy, avoid completing
prohibited assessed work, preserve the separation between students and
courses, and fail safely when the available evidence is incomplete. Fluency is
therefore insufficient: the authority for each action and the evidence for
each factual claim must remain inspectable.

This report investigates a Course Digital Twin, defined here as a
professor-configurable tutoring system grounded in approved course material
and constrained by explicit pedagogical and academic-integrity policies. The
intended product allows a professor to govern the sources and behaviour of a
course-specific tutor, evaluate it before publication, and withdraw or roll
back a release. Students interact only with an assigned published release and
receive either a grounded tutoring response or an explicit safe action such as
clarification, refusal, or abstention.

The main engineering difficulty is that these requirements cross several
system boundaries. Ingestion and retrieval must preserve permission,
provenance, and version identity. Generation must transform retrieved evidence
into claims with citations to the exact supporting source ranges. Tutoring
logic must adapt to a learner without allowing a language model to control
identity, policy, delivery, cost, publication, or learner-state authority.
Persistence, recovery, and rollback must preserve the same constraints after a
restart or provider failure. An evaluation limited to an isolated model or a
successful demonstration would not detect failures across these boundaries.

The project consequently uses an evaluation-first method. Baselines and
candidate methods are compared on versioned datasets using predefined quality
metrics, hard safety gates, operational measurements, and explicit
\emph{Keep}, \emph{Refine}, \emph{Go Deeper}, \emph{Drop}, or
\emph{No Release} decisions. Favorable results are retained alongside
unfavorable, corrected, and invalid runs. This makes it possible to distinguish
the local and component-level controls that passed their gates from integrated
LLM-backed candidates that remained below the required standard.

\subsection{Research questions and contribution}
\label{sec:research-questions}

The report addresses four questions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item How can source permission, teaching policy, academic-integrity rules,
        and publication authority be converted into enforceable controls for a
        professor-configurable Course Digital Twin?
  \item Which retrieval and evidence-representation approaches best support
        course-isolated, fully grounded factual answers under predefined
        quality and operational gates?
  \item Can a governed tutoring runtime preserve safe actions, citation
        lineage, learner-state authority, persistence, recovery, and rollback
        across reactive and proactive tutoring flows?
  \item Why can an integrated autonomous LLM-backed candidate remain
        ineligible for release even when deterministic, component-level, and
        local operational controls succeed?
\end{enumerate}

The provisional contribution is twofold. First, the project provides an
inspectable architecture that separates model proposals from application-owned
authority over evidence, policy, state, publication, delivery, and rollback.
Second, it provides comparative evidence showing that successful governance
and operational controls do not compensate for failures in factual grounding,
academic-integrity action, or exact claim-to-citation lineage. The resulting
release decision is treated as an academic result rather than hidden behind a
successful demonstration.
